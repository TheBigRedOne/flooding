\begin{abstract}

Producer mobility in systems that use Named Data Networking (NDN) protocols can lead to increased latency and packet loss due to slow routing convergence and stale forwarding state. This affects applications, such as live video streaming and video conferencing, where even a brief interruption in packet delivery can degrade user experience. To address this, we propose \textit{OptoFlood}, a controllable flooding mechanism that supports both Data and Interest delivery immediately following producer movement events, to rapidly establish temporary bidirectional communication paths. In OptoFlood, mobility-marked packets are disseminated with bounded scope, deduplication, and rate limiting, while intermediate nodes create short-lived forwarding hints via a temporary FIB (TFIB). When forwarding fails due to both FIB and TFIB misses, the Forwarder triggers a hop-limited flooding procedure to discover viable paths. OptoFlood also accelerates global routing convergence through Fast-LSA. Our evaluation demonstrates that OptoFlood effectively reduces latency and packet loss caused by producer mobility while maintaining reasonable network overhead.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Paragraph 1: Motivation.
Named Data Networking (NDN) is designed around named content rather than host addresses \cite{jacobson:2009:named-content, zhang:2014:ndn}. Name-based forwarding, content delivery, and in-network caching are therefore integrated into the network architecture.

% Paragraph 2: Specific problem.
However, producer mobility remains difficult to support with current NDN routing. After a producer changes its attachment point, newly issued Interests may continue to follow stale forwarding state towards the previous attachment point until routing information is updated. Data returned for Interests received before the movement enters the network from the new attachment point and may no longer enter the reverse forwarding path established by those Interests. These forwarding failures can interrupt service and leave Interests unsatisfied. This is particularly problematic for latency-sensitive applications such as live video streaming and video conferencing. Our baseline measurements show that native NDN can still require tens of seconds to restore service after a hand-off.

% Paragraph 3: Contributions.
In this paper, we introduce OptoFlood to restore packet delivery after producer movement and to accelerate ordinary routing convergence. Immediate Service Recovery uses Data returned for Interests received before movement to reach the existing reverse forwarding path. Bounded temporary forwarding then allows subsequent Interests to reach the producer before ordinary routing has converged. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence uses observations from recovery traffic to accelerate ordinary routing convergence in service-relevant parts of the network. Event-Driven Routing Convergence accelerates ordinary NLSR operations after mobility-related topology changes by reducing avoidable control-plane waiting. The three mechanisms are complementary and operate concurrently.

% Paragraph 4: Comparison to related work.
In previous NDN mobility solutions, KITE restores producer reachability using rendezvous-based hop-by-hop traces \cite{zhang:2018:kite}, while MAP-Me uses hop-by-hop data-plane forwarding updates without relying on global routing updates \cite{auge:2018:map-me}. OptoFlood similarly restores packet delivery before routing has converged, but additionally accelerates NLSR convergence after the same mobility event. Temporary forwarding remains separate from routing authority. Recovery observations are used to prioritise service-relevant convergence, while signed NLSR state remains authoritative. This combination addresses both service continuity during convergence and the time required for ordinary routing to converge.

% Paragraph 5: Roadmap.
We structure the remainder of this paper as follows. Section II describes NDN forwarding and routing and characterises the producer-mobility problem, including baseline routing-convergence behaviour. Section III presents the OptoFlood design and its three complementary mechanisms for service recovery and routing convergence. Section IV describes the experimental methodology and evaluation of OptoFlood. Section V discusses related work. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper.

\section{Producer Mobility in NDN}
\label{sec:problem}

Producer mobility in NDN can disrupt both Interest forwarding and Data return. After a producer changes its attachment point, newly issued Interests may continue to be forwarded towards the old attachment point until routing information is updated. Data returned for Interests received before the movement enters the network from the new attachment point. It may therefore no longer enter the reverse forwarding path established by the original Interests.

\subsection{NDN Forwarding and Routing}
\label{sec:problem:forwarding}

NDN packet delivery relies on the interaction between the data plane and the control plane. In the data plane, packets are forwarded using content names and forwarding state maintained by NDN forwarders \cite{xylomenos:2014:icn-survey, zhang:2014:ndn}. In NDN, communication relies on two core packet types: Interest Packets, sent by consumers to request data by specifying the content name, and Data Packets, returned in response to Interests and carrying the requested content and related metadata  \cite{jacobson:2009:named-content, zhang:2014:ndn}. Both use Type-Length-Value (TLV) encoding \cite{ndn:packet-format}.

    \caption{NDN Packets Processing Flow \csp{text in this figure is too small}}
    \label{fig:NDN Packets Processing Flow}
\end{figure}

NDN forwarders (Figure \ref{fig:NDN Packets Processing Flow}) maintain a Content Store that caches Data packets; a Pending Interest Table (PIT) that records state for forwarded Interests and enables matching Data to be returned along the reverse path; and a Forwarding Information Base (FIB) that guides Interest forwarding \cite{yi:2013:stateful-forwarding, zhang:2014:ndn}.

When an Interest arrives at a forwarder, the forwarder checks the Content Store and returns cached Data if available. Otherwise, the forwarder checks the PIT to determine whether a matching Interest is already pending. If the Interest is new, it is forwarded based on the FIB.

In the control plane, the Named-Data Link State Routing protocol (NLSR) disseminates link-state and prefix-reachability information using Link State Advertisements (LSAs). NLSR uses this information to calculate routes, which are subsequently reflected in the FIB. Following a topology change, NLSR verifies the affected adjacency state and constructs updated routing information. The update is disseminated through NLSR's synchronisation process, after which routes are recalculated and the forwarding state is updated \cite{hoque:2013:nlsr}. Neighbour verification, Adj-LSA construction, synchronisation, and route calculation may introduce control-plane waiting.

\label{sec:problem:mobility}

When a producer changes its attachment point, ordinary routing must reflect the changed topology before newly issued Interests can reach the producer at its new attachment. During this convergence period, newly issued Interests may continue to follow stale forwarding state towards the producer's previous attachment point.

Interests received before the movement may leave PIT state along the pre-movement path. Data returned for those Interests after the movement enters the network at the new attachment point. It may therefore no longer enter the reverse forwarding path established by the original Interests.

These forwarding failures can have several consequences:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Service Disruption:} Until ordinary routing reflects the producer's new attachment point, newly issued Interests may not reach the producer. This can interrupt service during routing convergence.
    \item \textbf{Unsatisfied Interests:} Interests may expire unsatisfied when newly issued Interests cannot reach the producer or when Data returned for pre-movement Interests cannot reach the requester.
    \item \textbf{Increased Retransmissions:} Unsatisfied Interests may be retransmitted, increasing bandwidth and processing overhead.
\end{itemize}

Consider a live-video producer that moves between access nodes while maintaining an ongoing stream. Ordinary routing can eventually restore reachability after each hand-off, but convergence takes time. If the producer changes attachment again before routing has converged from the previous movement, the network may still contain forwarding and routing state that reflects an earlier attachment. Ordinary NDN routing is therefore poorly suited to producer-mobility scenarios in which attachment changes occur on a timescale comparable to or shorter than routing convergence.

The baseline experiments use ordinary NDN forwarding and NLSR with OptoFlood disabled. They first quantify the service disruption caused by producer movement under native routing. We then reduce the main NLSR and PSync timing parameters to determine whether more aggressive routing configuration alone can provide sufficiently fast recovery, and to observe the associated control-plane cost.

Several stages of ordinary routing convergence are affected by configurable timing parameters. We vary four of these parameters in the baseline experiments. The NLSR Hello interval controls periodic neighbour verification. The Adj-LSA build delay controls how long NLSR waits before constructing a scheduled adjacency LSA. The routing calculation delay controls how long NLSR waits before a scheduled route recalculation. The PSync Sync Interest lifetime affects the timing of the synchronisation process used to disseminate routing updates \cite{zhang:2017:psync}.

The four timing parameters are progressively reduced across five configurations. Values are reported in the order of Hello interval, Adj-LSA build delay, routing calculation delay, and PSync Sync Interest lifetime. G0 uses the default values of 60 s, 10 s, 15 s, and 60 s. G1 uses 54 s, 9 s, 14 s, and 54 s; G2 uses 48 s, 8 s, 12 s, and 48 s; G3 uses 42 s, 7 s, 10 s, and 42 s; and G4 uses 36 s, 6 s, 9 s, and 36 s.

These baseline measurements use the three-layer access-network topology shown in Figure~\ref{exp_topo}. A fixed consumer retrieves a stream from a producer that performs hand-offs across the access nodes during the run.

Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_nlsr_disruption_comparison} compares the observed service disruption across the five configurations. G4 reduces disruption relative to the default configuration (G0) for some hand-offs. However, disruption still remains on the order of tens of seconds.

It is calculated as the total application bytes forwarded by relay nodes in the core, aggregation, and access layers, divided by the application Data bytes delivered to the consumer over the complete run. A higher value reflects more network transmission effort per delivered byte. Its absolute value is influenced by the consumer-to-producer path length, and hence by the topology and the producer's sequence of attachment points. We therefore treat it as a forwarding-overhead comparison under the same movement pattern. Across the tested configurations, the forwarding-cost ratio remains broadly similar, while routing-control traffic increases as the timing parameters are reduced.

\section{OptoFlood Design}
\label{sec:design}

When a producer changes its attachment point, Data returned for Interests received before the movement may no longer enter the existing reverse forwarding path, while newly issued Interests may continue to follow ordinary routing towards the old attachment. OptoFlood addresses both effects with three complementary mechanisms that can operate concurrently.

Immediate Service Recovery restores packet delivery and establishes temporary producer-facing forwarding for subsequent Interests. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence uses consumer-facing faces identified during recovery to prioritise ordinary routing convergence in service-relevant parts of the network. Event-Driven Routing Convergence advances the timing of ordinary NLSR operations after the mobility event. Temporary recovery state supports packet delivery and convergence prioritisation, while ordinary routing decisions continue to derive from validated NLSR state.

\subsection{Immediate Service Recovery}
\label{sec:design:isr}

After a producer changes its attachment point, a local attachment-change indication reaches the producer-side NFD. That event starts a new mobility generation for the producer prefix. The producer-side NFD then records the names of application Interests for that prefix that were received before the movement and remain unsatisfied.

The producer later returns Data in response to those Interests. When the Data enters the producer-side NFD from the local producer face, the NFD checks whether its name is in the recorded set. If it matches, the NFD marks the packet as recovery Data and associates it with the current mobility generation. The matched name is then removed from the recorded set.

Forwarding-Guided Data Recovery uses the ordinary FIB to direct recovery Data towards the pre-movement forwarding path. At each router, the recovery Data follows the FIB next hop for the producer prefix, excluding the face on which it arrived. If the Data reaches a router with a matching PIT entry, the router forwards it on the PIT in-record faces established by the corresponding Interest. The recovery mark is removed before the Data continues along this ordinary PIT return towards the consumer.

If a router has no FIB next hop for the producer prefix, the FIB can no longer guide the recovery Data towards the pre-movement forwarding path. In this case, the recovery Data can be flooded over eligible neighbouring point-to-point faces, subject to a bounded hop limit.

When a router receives recovery Data on a remote point-to-point face, it records that ingress face as a temporary next hop towards the producer for the corresponding prefix. OptoFlood stores this state in a Temporary FIB (TFIB), separately from the ordinary FIB. The stored face points towards the producer. The local producer application face is not recorded. While a TFIB entry is active, later Interests for the prefix are forwarded on that face before ordinary FIB forwarding, so they can reach the producer before ordinary routing has converged.

Recovery Data is associated with a mobility generation, ordered per producer prefix. TFIB state learned from a newer generation replaces state learned from an older generation for that prefix. Recovery Data from a stale generation does not overwrite newer TFIB state.

To provide a recovery opportunity when no suitable application Interest is pending at the time of movement, the consumer side continuously maintains guard Interests under the producer prefix. The consumer-side control process issues these Interests, and they are kept pending at the producer side during ordinary operation. Guard Interests do not request application payload. They use a dedicated name under the producer prefix and are not included in the recorded set of pre-movement application Interest names. After producer movement, the producer side returns empty-payload guard Data carrying the current mobility generation. Guard Data enters the same Forwarding-Guided Data Recovery process as application recovery Data.
When the exact ordinary FIB entry for the producer prefix selects the same next-hop direction as the active TFIB entry for a stability period, forwarding control returns to the ordinary FIB and forwarding strategy. This agreement does not necessarily mean that routing has fully stabilised, because subsequent routing updates may still withdraw the route or change its next hop. The TFIB entry is therefore retained temporarily in standby. If the ordinary route becomes unavailable or diverges from the recovered direction during this period, the TFIB entry can resume active forwarding.

\subsection{Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence}
\label{sec:design:sprc}

A forwarder records local ServiceBranch state for the producer prefix from application Interests and Data already processed during recovery. This state maps the producer prefix to a set of consumer-facing point-to-point faces. An Interest for the producer prefix can identify such a face when it arrives on that face and is forwarded towards the producer using temporary forwarding. Returning application Data can also identify consumer-facing faces through the corresponding PIT in-records. Guard traffic does not create ServiceBranch state. ServiceBranch state does not forward packets and is not a route. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence uses these faces to prioritise where ordinary routing convergence should progress first.

After a router's NLSR generates and installs an updated Adj-LSA for its local adjacency state, it sends an availability indication on its active NLSR adjacencies. The indication is a control Interest. Its name identifies the ordinary Adj-LSA version, including the origin, type, sequence number, and producer-prefix context. It does not carry the Adj-LSA body. It tells the receiving router that this ordinary Adj-LSA version can be obtained through the face on which the indication arrived.

The receiving router's NLSR receives the availability indication on a particular face. It extracts the advertised Adj-LSA origin, type, and sequence number, and looks up the corresponding ordinary Adj-LSA in its local LSDB. If no such LSA exists, or if the local sequence number is older, that NLSR sends an ordinary LSA Interest through the ingress face of the availability indication and fetches the ordinary signed Adj-LSA. After validation, the Adj-LSA is installed in the ordinary LSDB. Normal NLSR route calculation then processes that LSDB state. If the same or a newer sequence is already present, the router does not fetch the advertised Adj-LSA again.

The availability indication is not routing state. Routing changes only after the ordinary signed Adj-LSA has been fetched, validated, installed in the LSDB, and processed by normal NLSR route calculation.

Once the advertised Adj-LSA is present in the local LSDB, the router can send the same form of availability indication on its ServiceBranch faces for the producer prefix. These faces are consumer-facing. Routers farther towards the consumers can therefore fetch the ordinary Adj-LSA without waiting for the normal synchronisation schedule. The availability indication is what is propagated. The ordinary signed Adj-LSA is fetched separately.

To give Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence a bounded head start, the updated ordinary Adj-LSA is installed locally before its publication through ordinary NLSR synchronisation. That publication is delayed briefly while the locally installed LSA remains immediately available to Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence. Multiple updates during this interval are coalesced, and the latest version is then published through ordinary NLSR synchronisation.

\subsection{Event-Driven Routing Convergence}
\label{sec:design:edrc}

Ordinary NLSR verifies neighbour reachability through periodic Hello exchanges, although other native events may also cause Hello transmission. After a local attachment-change indication reaches the NLSR on the producer node, that NLSR promptly starts ordinary Hello verification of its configured neighbours that are active or have a face available for verification. This avoids relying only on periodic neighbour verification. Within this verification episode, neighbour status changes only after receipt of validated Hello Data or exhaustion of the ordinary Hello retries.

Adjacency status changes discovered during this episode would normally wait for that NLSR's delayed Adj-LSA construction. While the episode is in progress, the producer-node NLSR holds construction of its own ordinary Adj-LSA, so that an early result cannot publish a partially updated local adjacency table. Adjacency changes are retained during the hold. When every targeted neighbour has a definitive reachable or unreachable result, an updated Adj-LSA is built immediately if the local adjacency state changed. If verification of any targeted neighbour remains inconclusive, the hold is released and ordinary delayed Adj-LSA construction resumes.

When NLSR uses PSync FullSync, the same NLSR can promptly re-express an ordinary FullSync Interest after an updated ordinary Adj-LSA sequence has been published through PSync, instead of relying only on the next scheduled re-expression. Subsequent LSA discovery and fetching continue through the ordinary NLSR mechanisms.

A router that receives a FullSync Interest compares the remote FullSync state represented by that Interest with its own local FullSync state. If the remote state contains updates that the router does not yet have, and the router has no Sync Data to return, it promptly re-expresses its own ordinary FullSync Interest. A neighbour holding those updates can then return ordinary Sync Data.

An installed ordinary Adj-LSA may change the adjacency set advertised by its origin router. Event-Driven Routing Convergence can then advance the ordinary route calculation instead of waiting for the configured routing calculation delay. If the change adds adjacencies, prompt calculation waits until each new link is declared in both endpoints' Adj-LSAs. A deletion-only change can request that calculation without waiting for reciprocal addition confirmation. For a mixed addition and deletion, only the newly added links require those reciprocal declarations. The calculation itself remains the ordinary NLSR routing calculation, and the resulting routes are installed through the standard NLSR forwarding-update path.

Event-Driven Routing Convergence changes the timing of these operations, not their routing semantics. Neighbour status still comes from ordinary Hello validation, and routing state still comes from ordinary signed Adj-LSAs processed through NLSR. Ordinary PSync and route calculation remain unchanged apart from when they are invoked.

\subsection{Recovery and Convergence Coordination}
\label{sec:design:coordination}

Immediate Service Recovery can restore packet delivery while ordinary routing is still converging. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence and Event-Driven Routing Convergence proceed in parallel with this recovery. Neither mechanism must complete before temporary recovery can maintain service continuity.

TFIB state provides a temporary producer-facing direction for subsequent Interests. ServiceBranch state identifies consumer-facing faces on which Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence prioritises ordinary routing convergence. Event-Driven Routing Convergence separately advances the timing of ordinary NLSR verification, synchronisation, and route calculation after mobility events. Neither TFIB nor ServiceBranch state determines an ordinary route. Ordinary routing decisions continue to derive from validated NLSR state.

After forwarding control returns to the ordinary FIB, the TFIB entry remains temporarily in standby so that recovery can resume if ordinary forwarding changes before routing has stabilised.

If an accelerated routing step cannot complete, convergence continues through the corresponding ordinary NLSR process. TFIB state is temporary and eventually expires. ServiceBranch state for the same prefix is removed when the corresponding TFIB state expires.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate OptoFlood in a real-time streaming scenario with local producer mobility. The objective is to determine whether temporary flooding and Fast-LSA can preserve service continuity during hand-off while keeping the additional forwarding and control traffic bounded.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Methodology}

We consider producer micro-mobility within a single access domain, where an ongoing real-time stream, generated by the producer in response to received Interest packets,  remains active while the producer changes its attachment point. This setting stresses the interval between the physical move and routing convergence, which is precisely where NDN producer mobility causes service interruption.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Producer Application}
We implement a typical NDN video producer with our mobility extensions.
Generated video Data packets have a freshness period of 10 seconds, permitting caching but preventing stale data from clogging the network, and carry a payload of synthetic data matching the size and timing of video content. The packets are digitally signed to ensure integrity and security.
Content prefixes are advertised via NLSR in the usual manner, with the extensions discussed in Section \ref{sec:solution}.

Upon detecting a change in its network attachment point, the producer flags the Data packets generated in response to Interests received before the move. The flagged packets carry the markers that drive controlled flooding and convergence acceleration as described in Section~\ref{sec:solution:data-flooding}. Immutable identifiers (Flood-ID and the new-face sequence number used for forwarding hints) are placed in signed Data MetaInfo extension TLVs; hop-by-hop controls (mobility indication and scoped hop limits) ride in the LP header and may be rewritten by intermediate forwarders.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Consumer Application}

The consumer issues Interests every 33~ms (30~fps) with freshness and a 6~s lifetime, validates returned Data using a trust schema, and logs basic metrics.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Network Topology}
The evaluation topology is a three-layer access network with one core node, two aggregation nodes, and six access nodes. The consumer is attached to one access node, while the producer is initially attached to a neighbouring access node and then moves across the access nodes during the run. Each hand-off is a one-hop move within the access network.

The links are configured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core to aggregation:} 1~Gbps, 1~ms.
    \item \textbf{Aggregation to access:} 500~Mbps, 5~ms.
    \item \textbf{Consumer/producer to access:} 100~Mbps, 5~ms.
\end{itemize}

The core, aggregation, and access nodes form the in-network forwarding fabric and are referred to throughout the evaluation as \textbf{relay nodes}; each one runs NFD and NLSR, forwards application Interests toward the producer through FIB lookups, and returns Data along the reverse PIT path to the consumer.

\subsubsection{Experiment Procedure and Measurement}

The experiments are run in Mini-NDN 0.7.0 \cite{lantz:2010:mininet}. We compare two configurations: baseline NDN forwarding and OptoFlood-enabled forwarding. Both use the same topology, applications, and sequence of attachment points. In each run, all nodes start NFD and NLSR. They are given 30~s to converge before the applications are launched. The producer then performs 16 hand-offs, completing two round trips across the access nodes. Consecutive hand-offs are separated by an interval drawn uniformly at random from 90 to 150~s. This spacing is long enough for each transient to be observed in full.

We record packet traces (\texttt{ndndump}) in three places: the consumer, the access switch adjacent to the old PoA, and the access switch adjacent to the new PoA for each hand-off.
 In addition, we retain packet traces on all relay and endpoint nodes for analysis.
The simulation runs as containers on a single AMD EPYC server with 64 cores, 128 threads, 512GB memory, running Debian 12 ``bookworm''.
Where cross-node timing is required, we compute relative times from the consumer's point of view.

We report four complementary views of performance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Throughput continuity:} delivered consumer throughput over time.
    \item \textbf{Service disruption time:} for each hand-off, the elapsed time at the consumer between the last Data reception before the move and the first Data reception after service resumes.
    \item \textbf{Unmet-interest ratio:} the number of first-time Interest names whose Data fails to arrive at the consumer within the 6~s application deadline, divided by the number of first-time Interest names issued in the same interval. We report this ratio inside the post-handoff windows (the union of 10~s intervals immediately following each move) and for the steady-state remainder of the run.
    \item \textbf{Forwarding overhead:} application relay load across the relay nodes, normalised by delivered Data bytes, together with a decomposition into explicit flooding traffic and NLSR control traffic.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Request Interval and Video Quality}
\label{sec:evaluation:extended:interval}

The request interval sets the rate at which the consumer requests successive frames, which we use as a proxy for stream quality. We hold the network delay at its main-evaluation value and vary the request interval over a representative range of 10 to 50~ms, with the producer generating one frame per request period. For live streaming, a Data packet is useful only before its playout deadline, and a late packet is equivalent to a lost one; the deadline represents the playout buffer. A frame counts as lost when its Data does not arrive within this deadline. The number of Interests in flight at a hand-off grows as the request interval shrinks, so a finer interval leaves more frames exposed to the convergence gap.

Figure~\ref{fig:ext_interval} reports OptoFlood across the request-interval range. Panel~(a) shows that the interruption time per-handover remains approximately flat. The recovery latency is set by the network--flooded Data and Interest propagation, then TFIB installation, and Fast-LSA--and is independent of the request rate. Panel~(b) shows that the deadline-missed frames per hand-off increase as the interval shrinks. A finer interval places more frames in flight during the convergence gap, and each frame past its deadline counts as lost. Panel~(c) reports the flood load per hand-off, separated into flooded Data and flooded Interest. The flood load increases as the interval shrinks, and the hop limit and per-producer rate limit bound it. Data flooding covers only the Interests pending at the producer when it moves, so its volume tracks the in-flight count. Interest flooding is a last resort that a forwarder issues when both the FIB and the TFIB miss, and it stops once the first flooded Data instals a TFIB entry or Fast-LSA restores the route. Sustained Interest flooding therefore arises only when the producer stays unreachable for an extended period. Across the tested range, the flood load remains localised to the recovery window and becomes significant only at the finest intervals.

\subsubsection{Multiple Consumers}
\label{sec:evaluation:extended:consumers}

We attach several consumers to the access network under two demand patterns. In the \emph{shared} pattern, all consumers view the same live stream. Two mechanisms then let them share the recovery: consumers requesting the same frame at the same time are aggregated in the PIT, and consumers lagging the live edge by less than the Data freshness period are served from the Content Store. The temporary forwarding state that OptoFlood instals from mobility-marked data is a per-prefix forwarder state, so it is shared as well. In the \emph{distinct} pattern, each consumer requests a separate stream from the same producer, so no content is shared, and each stream is recovered on its own prefix. We scale the number of consumers, and for the shared pattern we also vary the lag behind the live edge relative to the freshness period.

Under the shared pattern, the cost stays close to its single-consumer value, because the mobility-marked Data is flooded once per event and its copies populate the Content Store. Under the distinct pattern the cost grows with the number of consumers, because each separate stream carries its own pending Interests and is recovered on its own prefix. The contrast isolates the saving from content sharing: the per consumer cost falls with scale under the shared pattern and remains constant per consumer under the distinct pattern.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The management of producer mobility in NDN has attracted significant research attention \cite{abrar:2023:systematic, feng:2016:survey, zhang:2016:mobility-survey}. Various solutions have emerged, generally categorised according to their underlying mechanisms. Here, we summarise three representative approaches—KITE, AFIRM, and MAP-Me—and highlight their respective strengths and limitations.

\textbf{KITE} \cite{zhang:2018:kite} is a trace-based mobility support scheme that leverages an immobile rendezvous point and the stateful NDN forwarding plane. A mobile producer maintains soft state by exchanging authenticated Interest/Data messages that establish a hop-by-hop trace toward its latest attachment point, allowing subsequent Interests to follow the trace after reaching the rendezvous point. This approach reduces the reliance on slow global routing convergence and keeps data retrieval largely transparent to applications. However, KITE introduces a dependency on a reachable rendezvous point and periodic signalling to refresh soft state, which can become a scalability concern under frequent mobility or large numbers of mobile producers.

\textbf{AFIRM (Adaptive Forwarding-based Link Recovery for Mobility)} \cite{meddeb:2018:afirm} addresses producer mobility by repairing forwarding behaviour through adaptive forwarding and local recovery, aiming to re-establish usable paths without introducing a permanent anchor. By reacting to mobility-induced disruptions and adjusting forwarding decisions, AFIRM can reduce service interruption for localised micro-mobility. However, its effectiveness depends on the timely detection and stability of local recovery in highly dynamic conditions; frequent mobility may also introduce additional signalling and forwarding overhead.

\textbf{MAP-Me (Managing Anchor-less Producer Mobility)} \cite{auge:2016:map-me, auge:2018:map-me} provides anchor-less mobility support by handling producer location updates in the data plane. Upon producer movement, MAP-Me propagates update information hop-by-hop to re-direct affected forwarding paths, avoiding the delays associated with purely routing-plane convergence. MAP-Me is therefore well-suited to latency-sensitive micro-mobility scenarios. However, in frequent or high-intensity mobility, repeated updates can generate noticeable overhead and may require careful tuning to balance responsiveness against network cost.

In terms of route updates, KITE maintains soft-state traces rooted at a rendezvous point, while AFIRM focusses on local forwarding recovery, and MAP-Me proactively updates forwarding state in the data plane upon movement. All three differ fundamentally from baseline NDN deployments where producer relocation requires routing-plane updates (e.g., NLSR prefix re-advertisement) and subsequent global convergence.

\textbf{MoQ/QUICR (Media over QUIC)} \cite{jennings:2022:quicr-arch} proposes a relay-assisted publish/subscribe architecture over QUIC for low-latency media delivery. Producers publish named media objects, and consumers subscribe; relays can cache and fan out objects. Mobility is handled primarily via endpoint re-attachment (e.g., QUIC connection migration and re-subscription), offering fast application-level continuity. However, this is an overlay design: it does not rely on the PIT/ FIF state of NDN in the network, nor does it provide an in the network mechanism to rescue pending requests along disrupted forwarding paths after producer movement. Thus, MoQ/QUICR shares NDN-like ideas (naming and caching), but targets a different layer and problem scope.

In comparison, our OptoFlood solution offers a flood-based mechanism specifically designed for dynamic producer mobility scenarios, employing controlled flooding of \emph{both} Data and Interest packets immediately following producer movement. Unlike KITE, OptoFlood does not depend on a rendezvous/anchor node, avoiding central dependency in the forwarding workflow. Compared to AFIRM and MAP-Me, which focus on forwarding recovery or explicit update propagation, OptoFlood prioritises \emph{immediate} temporary reachability: (i) mobility-marked Data packets are locally flooded to rescue pending Interests on the old path, and (ii) forwarders trigger scoped Interest flooding when forwarding state is missing, to rapidly discover the producer’s new attachment point. Once a working face is confirmed, forwarders install short-lived TFIB state to quickly cut back to unicast. Finally, OptoFlood couples these temporary paths with the routing plane by triggering a short-lived, locally scoped Fast-LSA update via NLSR, accelerating convergence while allowing normal NLSR advertisements to eventually take over.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \textit{OptoFlood}, a controllable flooding mechanism for producer mobility in Named Data Networking. OptoFlood addresses the key weakness of routing-based mobility handling: after a producer handover, forwarding state and routing information can remain stale for seconds, during which Interests may be discarded and Data may fail to return along invalidated reverse paths. Rather than waiting for global convergence, OptoFlood provides immediate localised recovery by establishing temporary bidirectional reachability and then accelerating routing convergence through lightweight integration with NLSR.

OptoFlood combines two complementary mechanisms. First, mobility-marked \emph{Data packet flooding} disseminates Data generated around a handover with bounded scope and duplicate suppression, improving the likelihood that pending Interests issued before the move are satisfied without relying on global route updates. Second, \emph{Interest packet flooding} is triggered by forwarders only as a last resort, when both FIB and TFIB provide no usable next hop, and is confined by native hop limits and per-node de-duplication. Intermediate forwarders opportunistically install short-lived TFIB hints to quickly revert subsequent Interests back to unicast forwarding once a viable face is observed. Finally, OptoFlood couples these temporary paths to the control plane via a Fast-LSA trigger, installing short-lived routing entries through NLSR to shorten the overall convergence period while allowing standard NLSR advertisements to eventually take over.

Overall, OptoFlood aims to keep mobility recovery \emph{local, short-lived, and explicitly bounded}: flooding is scoped (hop-limited), suppressed (duplicate filtering), and constrained (rate limiting), while cryptographic integrity is preserved by keeping immutable identifiers inside signed Data MetaInfo extensions and leaving hop-by-hop controls in the LP header for in-network processing. These design choices make OptoFlood practical for latency-sensitive micro-mobility settings, where rapid continuity matters and mobility events occur frequently enough that waiting for global convergence alone is insufficient.

\subsection{Long Term Impact Assessment}

\subsubsection{Continuous optimisation of network performance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Efficiency Improvements:} Because OptoFlood restores temporary reachability quickly after handover, it can reduce the duration of ``blind'' retransmission phases where consumers repeatedly re-express Interests while forwarding state is stale. Over time, this can translate into smoother application performance and fewer bursts of recovery traffic during mobility.
    \item \textbf{Enhanced Reliability:} By providing a mechanism to rescue pending Interests and by enabling forwarders to cache short-lived forwarding hints (TFIB), the network becomes more resilient to transient disruptions caused by producer relocation, especially when mobility remains local (within a few hops) and frequent.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Resource Usage and Network Stability}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resource Optimization:} OptoFlood is based on explicitly bounded flooding with a short lifetime. TFIB entries and Fast-LSA updates are also short-lived, limiting long-term state growth and reducing the risk of persistent routing pollution. This provides a pragmatic balance between rapid recovery and overhead.
    \item \textbf{Stability Analysis:} Although flooding is controlled, its interaction with retransmission behaviour, caching, and routing updates can influence stability under high load or dense mobility. The throttling and de-duplication mechanisms in both the forwarding plane (FloodId/Nonce caches, rate limits) and the routing plane (Fast-LSA throttling) are therefore essential to prevent oscillation and excessive control traffic, and should be considered carefully in larger deployments.
\end{itemize}
