\begin{abstract}

Producer mobility in systems that use Named Data Networking (NDN) protocols can lead to increased latency and packet loss due to slow routing convergence and stale forwarding state. This affects applications, such as live video streaming and video conferencing, where even a brief interruption in packet delivery can degrade user experience. To address this, we propose \textit{OptoFlood}, a controllable flooding mechanism that supports both Data and Interest delivery immediately following producer movement events, to rapidly establish temporary bidirectional communication paths. In OptoFlood, mobility-marked packets are disseminated with bounded scope, deduplication, and rate limiting, while intermediate nodes create short-lived forwarding hints via a temporary FIB (TFIB). When forwarding fails due to both FIB and TFIB misses, the Forwarder triggers a hop-limited flooding procedure to discover viable paths. OptoFlood also accelerates global routing convergence through Fast-LSA. Our evaluation demonstrates that OptoFlood effectively reduces latency and packet loss caused by producer mobility while maintaining reasonable network overhead.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Paragraph 1: Motivation.
Named Data Networking (NDN) is an alternative network architecture that organises communication around named data rather than endpoint addresses \cite{jacobson:2009:named-content, zhang:2014:ndn}. Consumers request data by name, and the network can forward those requests and satisfy them from producers or in-network caches without requiring the consumer to identify a particular source host. This model is attractive for content-distribution applications, including those in which data is generated continuously by mobile endpoints. Examples include live video streaming and real-time conferencing, where a producer may change its network attachment while continuing to serve an ongoing stream.

% Paragraph 2: Specific problem.
NDN can accommodate producer movement through routing updates, but service recovery depends on how quickly those updates converge. After a producer changes its attachment point, newly issued Interest packets may continue to follow stale forwarding state towards the previous attachment until routing information is updated. Data packets sent after a producer changes its attachment point, but in response to Interest packets received prior to the change, may not be forwarded because they do not reach a forwarder with reverse-path forwarding state established by the pending Interest packets. These forwarding failures can interrupt packet delivery and leave Interest packets unsatisfied. Under native NDN routing, service disruption after a hand-off can extend to tens of seconds, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:problem:mobility}.

% Paragraph 3: Contributions.
In this paper, we introduce OptoFlood to restore packet delivery after producer movement and to accelerate convergence of the Named-Data Link State Routing (NLSR) protocol \cite{hoque:2013:nlsr}. This comprises three complementary mechanisms: \emph{Immediate Service Recovery}, which restores packet delivery and provides temporary forwarding during routing convergence (Section~\ref{sec:design:isr}); \emph{Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence}, which uses observations from recovery traffic to accelerate routing convergence in relevant parts of the network (Section~\ref{sec:design:sprc}); and \emph{Event-Driven Routing Convergence}, which reduces avoidable waiting in NLSR after mobility-related topology changes (Section~\ref{sec:design:edrc}).

% Paragraph 4: Comparison to related work.
Previous work on NDN producer mobility primarily restores producer reachability by establishing mobility-specific forwarding state. KITE \cite{zhang:2018:kite} creates rendezvous-based hop-by-hop traces and retransmits pending Interest packets when new forwarding information becomes available, while MAP-Me \cite{auge:2018:map-me} propagates hop-by-hop forwarding updates and uses local discovery to redirect Interest packets towards the producer's new attachment. OptoFlood takes a different approach to immediate recovery: Data packets sent in response to Interest packets received before movement are used to restore the outstanding exchange and to establish temporary producer-facing forwarding for subsequent Interest packets. OptoFlood then uses information obtained during recovery to prioritise NLSR routing convergence, while separate event-driven mechanisms reduce avoidable waiting in NLSR operations.

\todo{Update this paragraph after the KITE and MAP-Me reproduction experiments are complete.}
Our evaluation shows that OptoFlood reduces service recovery time after producer hand-off from tens of seconds under native NDN routing to around one hundred milliseconds. KITE and MAP-Me also provide rapid producer-mobility recovery through mobility-specific forwarding mechanisms. We evaluate OptoFlood against these approaches using service recovery, delivery reliability, and recovery overhead under the same mobility workload. OptoFlood additionally accelerates NLSR convergence after the same mobility event.

% Paragraph 5: Roadmap.
We structure the remainder of this paper as follows. Section II describes NDN forwarding and routing and characterises the producer-mobility problem, including baseline routing-convergence behaviour. Section III presents the OptoFlood design and its three complementary mechanisms for service recovery and routing convergence. Section IV describes the experimental methodology and evaluation of OptoFlood. Section V discusses related work. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper.

\section{Producer Mobility in NDN}
\label{sec:problem}

This section briefly outlines NDN forwarding and routing, then discusses the effects of producer mobility on packet delivery and routing convergence.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{NDN Forwarding and Routing}
\label{sec:problem:forwarding}

NDN packet delivery relies on the interaction between the data plane and the control plane. In the data plane, packets are forwarded using content names and forwarding state maintained by NDN forwarders \cite{xylomenos:2014:icn-survey, zhang:2014:ndn}. In NDN, communication relies on two core packet types: Interest packets, sent by consumers to request data by specifying the content name, and Data packets, returned in response to Interests and carrying the requested content and related metadata  \cite{jacobson:2009:named-content, zhang:2014:ndn}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{NDN_Packets_Processing_Flow.pdf}
    \caption{NDN Packets Processing Flow \csp{text in this figure is too small}}
    \label{fig:NDN Packets Processing Flow}
\end{figure}

NDN forwarders (Figure \ref{fig:NDN Packets Processing Flow}) maintain a Content Store that caches Data packets; a Pending Interest Table (PIT) that records state for forwarded Interest packets and enables matching Data packets to be returned along the reverse path; and a Forwarding Information Base (FIB) that guides Interest forwarding \cite{yi:2013:stateful-forwarding, zhang:2014:ndn}.

When an Interest packet arrives at a forwarder, the forwarder checks the Content Store and returns a cached Data packet if available. Otherwise, the forwarder checks the PIT to determine whether a matching Interest packet is already pending. If the Interest packet is new, it is forwarded based on the FIB. The FIB is populated from routing information produced by NLSR.

In the control plane, NLSR is a link-state routing protocol for NDN. Routers advertise local adjacency state using adjacency Link State Advertisements (Adj-LSAs) and name-prefix reachability using Name Link State Advertisements (Name-LSAs). Received LSAs are stored in a local Link State Database (LSDB), from which NLSR calculates routes \cite{hoque:2013:nlsr}.

NLSR periodically verifies configured neighbours using Hello exchanges. The Hello interval controls the timing of this periodic verification. A change in verified adjacency state causes NLSR to schedule construction of an updated Adj-LSA. The Adj-LSA build delay allows closely spaced adjacency changes to be collected before the updated LSA is constructed.

NLSR uses PSync \cite{zhang:2017:psync} to synchronise information about available LSA versions. A router that learns of a newer LSA version retrieves and validates the corresponding signed LSA before installing it in the LSDB. The PSync Sync Interest lifetime affects the timing of FullSync re-expression. After new topology information is installed, NLSR schedules route calculation and subsequently updates the FIB. The routing calculation delay determines how long a scheduled calculation waits before it runs.

\subsection{Producer Mobility and Routing Convergence}
\label{sec:problem:mobility}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{NDN_Producer_Mobility_Problem.pdf}
	\caption{NDN Producer Mobility Problem \ryo{should this not be side-by-side with the solution Fig.\ref{fig:NDN Producer Mobility Problem Solution}?} \csp{text in this figure is too small}}
    \label{fig:NDN Producer Mobility Problem}
\end{figure}

When a producer changes its attachment point, routing must converge to reflect the changed topology before newly issued Interest packets can reach the producer at its new attachment point. During this convergence period, newly issued Interest packets will continue to follow stale forwarding state towards the producer's previous attachment point.

Interest packets received before the movement leave PIT state along the pre-movement path. Data packets sent in response to those Interest packets after the movement enter the network at the new attachment point. Unless the new attachment point lies on the previously established reverse forwarding path towards the requesting consumers, such Data packets will not be correctly delivered.

These forwarding failures have three consequences for the mobility evaluation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slow Service Recovery:} Fresh post-handoff Interest packets may continue to follow stale forwarding state and fail to reach the producer until a usable request path is restored.
    \item \textbf{Content Loss:} During this interval, newly issued Interest packets can remain unsatisfied, while Data packets generated for pre-movement Interest packets may fail to follow the established reverse path. In the live-stream workload, these forwarding failures can result in missing content frames.
    \item \textbf{Slow Routing Convergence and Control Cost:} NLSR must detect the changed adjacencies, disseminate the updated topology state, recalculate routes, and install the resulting forwarding state. More aggressive routing timers can shorten parts of this process, but they can also increase sustained routing-control traffic.
\end{itemize}

These consequences motivate the evaluation metrics. Service Recovery Time measures request-path recovery, while Content Loss Fraction measures delivery reliability over each mobility event. NLSR control rate measures sustained native routing-control traffic. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:routing} evaluates service-path and network-wide routing convergence using the FIB-installation endpoint. For OptoFlood, Recovery Flooding Volume quantifies the network-wide data-plane volume of flood-marked recovery traffic.

Consider a live-video producer that moves between access nodes while maintaining an ongoing stream. NLSR routing can eventually restore reachability after each hand-off, but convergence takes time. If the producer changes attachment again before routing has converged from the previous movement, the network may still contain forwarding and routing state that reflects an earlier attachment. Native NDN routing is therefore poorly suited to producer-mobility scenarios in which attachment changes occur on a timescale comparable to or shorter than routing convergence.

To demonstrate this, we measure service recovery, content loss, routing convergence, and routing-control cost under native NDN forwarding and routing. We first use the default NLSR and PSync configuration, and then repeat the same experiment with progressively reduced timing parameters: the NLSR Hello interval, Adj-LSA build delay, routing calculation delay, and PSync Sync Interest lifetime. This tests whether more aggressive routing configuration alone can provide sufficiently fast recovery and shows the control-plane cost of doing so. \section{OptoFlood Design}
\label{sec:design}

OptoFlood addresses the producer-mobility issues described in Section~\ref{sec:problem} with three complementary mechanisms that can operate concurrently. \emph{Immediate Service Recovery} restores packet delivery and establishes temporary forwarding for subsequent Interest packets. \emph{Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence} uses forwarding observations made during recovery to accelerate routing convergence in service-relevant parts of the network. \emph{Event-Driven Routing Convergence} accelerates NLSR routing convergence by reducing avoidable control-plane waiting after producer movement.

\subsection{Immediate Service Recovery}
\label{sec:design:isr}

Immediate Service Recovery separates endpoint mobility control from forwarding-plane recovery. The live-stream applications contain no OptoFlood-specific mobility logic, while a standalone OptoFlood daemon runs at the producer and consumer endpoints. The producer-side daemon detects attachment changes and triggers recovery, while the consumer-side daemon maintains the Guard Interest described below. The forwarding actions themselves are implemented in OptoFlood extensions to NFD, which process recovery Data and maintain temporary forwarding state.

When the producer changes its attachment point, the producer-side daemon detects the change and advances the mobility generation. It then arms the local NFD for the data prefix. Some Interest packets received before the move may still be pending when the attachment changes. Data packets subsequently generated for those requests are treated as recovery Data and carry the current mobility generation.

The producer's NFD and subsequent relay forwarders apply Forwarding-Guided Data Recovery to Data packets marked as recovery Data, using the FIB direction normally used by Interest packets. Recovery forwarding does not replace PIT-based Data delivery. After the recovery-forwarding step, a matching PIT entry can also return the Data packet towards the requesting consumer. If the FIB entry has no next hops, the forwarder falls back to neighbouring point-to-point interfaces, excluding the incoming interface, local interfaces, and interfaces represented by matching PIT state. In this case, the current recovery HopLimit restricts forwarding to one additional hop.

At each forwarder, the interface on which recovery Data arrives identifies a direction back towards the producer's new attachment point. OptoFlood records this direction and the associated mobility generation number separately from the FIB in a Temporary FIB (TFIB). While a TFIB entry is active, subsequent Interest packets for the data prefix follow this temporary direction. When recovery Data updates TFIB state, the forwarder compares its mobility generation number with the value stored in the existing entry. State associated with a higher generation number can replace state from a lower generation number. An earlier generation number cannot overwrite a later entry.

Recovery normally relies on an unsatisfied Interest packet remaining from before the move. When application traffic does not provide such an Interest packet, the consumer-side OptoFlood daemon periodically re-expresses a Guard Interest under a dedicated name within the data prefix so that a guard remains pending at the producer. Guard Interest packets do not request application payload. After producer movement, the producer-side daemon returns empty-payload Guard Data carrying the current mobility generation. Guard Data enters the same Forwarding-Guided Data Recovery process and can therefore establish the same temporary forwarding state.

When the FIB consistently selects the same forwarding direction as the active TFIB entry for the data prefix, forwarding control returns to the FIB. This agreement does not necessarily mean that routing has fully stabilised, because later routing updates may still withdraw the route or change its next hop. The TFIB entry therefore remains temporarily available as a standby fallback. If the FIB subsequently becomes unusable or changes direction before the temporary state expires, the TFIB entry can resume active forwarding.

\subsection{Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence}
\label{sec:design:sprc}

During temporary recovery, application traffic identifies the network branches carrying the affected service between the producer and its consumers. We refer to these as service-relevant branches. At each forwarder, OptoFlood records the interfaces leading towards consumers on these branches as ServiceBranch state for the data prefix. An interface is recorded when an application Interest packet arrives from that direction and is forwarded towards the producer using the TFIB. Returning application Data packets can identify the same interface through the corresponding PIT state. Guard traffic does not create ServiceBranch state because it is recovery-control traffic and does not represent application-service demand. The recorded interfaces identify where Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence makes updated routing information available first.

After NLSR generates and installs an updated Adj-LSA for its local adjacency state, OptoFlood sends an availability Interest to each active NLSR neighbour. An availability Interest is a one-hop control Interest that announces that a particular Adj-LSA version is available from the sender. Its name identifies the Adj-LSA origin, type, sequence number, and data-prefix context. The interface on which the Interest arrives identifies the neighbour from which that LSA version can be retrieved. The availability Interest is satisfied by an empty signed Data packet; it does not itself retrieve the Adj-LSA. The signed Adj-LSA remains the routing information used by NLSR.

An NLSR instance receiving an availability Interest compares the advertised Adj-LSA version with the version held in its LSDB. If the LSA is missing or older, it retrieves the signed Adj-LSA from the neighbour through the interface on which the availability Interest arrived. After validation, the LSA is installed in the LSDB and NLSR schedules route calculation. If the same version is already present, no retrieval is required and the availability Interest can continue along the recorded ServiceBranch interfaces. If a newer version is already present, the older availability Interest is not propagated further.

Once the advertised Adj-LSA version is available locally, the NLSR instance can propagate the same availability Interest through its recorded ServiceBranch interfaces towards affected consumers. Routers on these service-relevant branches can therefore obtain the updated Adj-LSA before the same update reaches them through NLSR synchronisation.

Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence gives this service-relevant dissemination a bounded head start over NLSR synchronisation. An updated Adj-LSA is made locally available before its synchronisation announcement. That announcement is delayed briefly, after which the latest locally installed version is published through NLSR synchronisation. Multiple updates during this interval are coalesced.

\subsection{Event-Driven Routing Convergence}
\label{sec:design:edrc}

NLSR verifies neighbour reachability through periodic Hello exchanges. Event-Driven Routing Convergence uses a producer attachment change to trigger immediate Hello verification of configured neighbours that can be verified. Neighbour status changes only after validated Hello is received or the existing Hello retries are exhausted. A validation failure does not establish either result.

During this verification process, construction of the producer-side router's updated Adj-LSA is deferred. This prevents an early verification result from publishing a partially updated adjacency state. When all targeted neighbours have a definitive reachable or unreachable result, an updated Adj-LSA is built immediately if the local adjacency state has changed. If any result remains inconclusive, the hold is released and scheduled Adj-LSA construction resumes.

When NLSR uses PSync, Event-Driven Routing Convergence can prompt a FullSync exchange after an updated Adj-LSA sequence has been published. This avoids waiting for the next scheduled re-expression. An NLSR instance that learns through FullSync that it is missing advertised updates can likewise request synchronisation promptly. LSA retrieval, validation, and installation then continue through the NLSR process.

After an updated Adj-LSA is installed, Event-Driven Routing Convergence can also request route calculation without waiting for the configured calculation delay. If the update adds an adjacency, prompt calculation waits until the new link is advertised by both endpoint routers. A deletion-only update can proceed without this reciprocal confirmation. For a mixed update, only the added links require it. The calculation itself remains the NLSR routing calculation, and the resulting routes are installed through the standard forwarding-update process. Event-Driven Routing Convergence therefore accelerates NLSR by advancing existing verification, synchronisation, and route-calculation operations without changing their routing semantics.

\subsection{Recovery and Convergence Coordination}
\label{sec:design:coordination}

The three OptoFlood mechanisms address different stages of recovery after producer movement. Immediate Service Recovery restores packet delivery before routing has converged and, in doing so, reveals forwarding directions associated with the affected service. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence uses these observations to make updated routing information available first along the service-relevant branches identified during recovery. Event-Driven Routing Convergence operates at the broader NLSR control plane, reducing the waiting involved in neighbour verification, synchronisation, and route calculation.

These mechanisms therefore provide complementary forms of acceleration. Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence gives spatial priority to the parts of the network currently carrying the affected service, while Event-Driven Routing Convergence reduces control-plane delay more generally. A router does not need to lie on a recorded service-relevant branch to benefit from Event-Driven Routing Convergence. Routing convergence can therefore continue beyond the paths identified by recovery traffic.

Together, the mechanisms provide a progression from immediate packet recovery, through prioritised convergence on service-relevant paths, to accelerated NLSR convergence across the broader network. The three mechanisms can operate concurrently. Temporary recovery can maintain packet delivery while routing converges to reflect the producer's new attachment point.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate OptoFlood on the producer-mobility workload introduced in Section~\ref{sec:problem:mobility}. We measure request-path recovery and content loss after each hand-off, and routing convergence on the service path and across the wider routing domain. We also quantify the associated recovery-flooding and routing-control overhead.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation:methodology}

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup and Procedure}

The experiments are run in Mini-NDN 0.7.0 \cite{lantz:2010:mininet}. We consider producer micro-mobility within a single access domain for a continuously generated, delay-sensitive live-media stream. The producer continues to serve the stream while changing its attachment point, stressing the interval between physical movement and routing convergence.

The evaluation uses the three-layer access-network topology shown in Figure~\ref{exp_topo}. It has one core node, two aggregation nodes, and six access nodes. The consumer remains attached to acc1. The producer is initially attached to the neighbouring access node acc2 and then moves from one access node to the next. The links are configured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core to aggregation:} 1~Gbps, 1~ms.
    \item \textbf{Aggregation to access:} 500~Mbps, 5~ms.
    \item \textbf{Consumer/producer to access:} 100~Mbps, 5~ms.
\end{itemize}

The core, aggregation, and access nodes form the in-network forwarding fabric and are referred to throughout the evaluation as \textbf{relay nodes}. Each relay runs NFD and NLSR, forwards application Interest packets towards the producer through FIB lookups, and returns Data along the reverse PIT path to the consumer.

The workload emulates a continuously generated, delay-sensitive live-media stream. The consumer requests one-segment frames at 20~ms intervals and maintains a small look-ahead of outstanding Interest packets, adapting its request window to the measured round-trip time. Interest packets use MustBeFresh.

We use the same topology, workload, attachment sequence, and hand-off procedure for native NDN and OptoFlood. OptoFlood retains the default NLSR and PSync timing while enabling all three mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:design}. Native NDN uses the G0--G4 timer profiles defined in Table~\ref{tab:baseline_configurations}; G0 is the reference for the direct OptoFlood comparison. Each configuration uses five independent runs of eight hand-offs, giving 40 mobility-event observations per configuration. The attachment sequence is acc2--acc3--acc4--acc5--acc6--acc5--acc4--acc3--acc2, returning to the initial attachment after one round trip. The interval between consecutive hand-offs is $90 + U(0,60)$~s, where $U(0,60)$ is a uniform draw from 0 to 60~s. Each run draws its own intervals, so the configurations share the sequence and the interval distribution.

In each run, all nodes start NFD and NLSR and are given 30~s for initial convergence. The producer links to acc3--acc6 are then taken down, leaving acc2 as the only producer attachment. The applications are then launched. A further interval from the same distribution elapses before the first hand-off. Packet captures are retained on every endpoint and relay node, together with the NLSR and NFD logs and the hand-off records used for the routing measurements.

\subsubsection{Measurement}
\label{sec:evaluation:measurement}

Each mobility event begins at a hand-off. For every hand-off except the last, the event interval is $[t_i, t_{i+1})$. The final event ends at the end of the packet capture.

For each mobility event, we measure service recovery and content delivery using SRT and CLF, and recovery and control overhead using RFV and NLSR control rate. Routing acceleration is evaluated using service-path and network-wide FIB convergence. Complete Mobility-Topology Update Latency and Service-Path LSA Lead provide supporting timing evidence for this convergence process.

Service Recovery Time (SRT) measures restoration of the request path. It is the time from the hand-off until the producer receives the first fresh normal-content Interest packet issued by the consumer after the hand-off. The consumer transmission and producer receipt are matched by content name, so the endpoint marks the first successful post-handoff request at the producer's new attachment. If no qualifying Interest packet reaches the producer before the event boundary, SRT is right-censored at the event length. The censoring bound is not treated as an observed SRT and is excluded from the numerical box statistics.

Content Loss Fraction (CLF) is computed over the whole mobility event. Nominal frame opportunities equal the event duration divided by the 20~ms request interval. A delivered frame is a unique normal live-stream frame received by the consumer during the event; repeated copies of the same frame count once. Guard, meta, NLSR, and management traffic are excluded. CLF is one minus the ratio of delivered unique frames to nominal frame opportunities.

Recovery Flooding Volume (RFV) is the recovery-induced flooding volume on the data plane, summed over the event and reported per hand-off. It counts sender-egress bytes of flood-marked recovery transmissions. An Interest transmission is included when it carries the OptoFlood HopLimit marker. A Data transmission is included when it carries an LP MobilityFlag or an LP OptoHopLimit. A MetaInfo FloodId remaining on a later copy does not, by itself, identify that copy as a flooded transmission. Sender-egress copies at different nodes are counted separately because they represent distinct link transmissions. RFV is not divided by the event duration. Figures show it in kilobytes per hand-off.

NLSR control rate is the network-wide sum of sender-egress NLSR bytes during the event, divided by the event duration. The unit is bytes per second.

For forwarding purposes, we treat routing as converged once the correct post-handoff route has been installed in the FIB. Updated NLSR state does not affect packet forwarding until this installation is complete. The endpoint is NFD's confirmation that the correct \texttt{/LiveStream} route derived from NLSR is installed. For service-path routing convergence, we consider the affected routers whose next hop must change along the path from the consumer to the producer's new attachment. The convergence time is the latest correct FIB installation among these routers. Network-wide routing convergence applies the same endpoint to all affected routers in the routing domain and takes the latest correct FIB installation. The producer originates the prefix and is outside this installation measurement. If any required router has not completed installation before the event boundary, the event is retained as right-censored, and the boundary is not used as a convergence time.

Two further quantities are used in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:routing}. Complete Mobility-Topology Update Latency is the time from the hand-off until the producer-side router constructs the updated Adj-LSA after both the former and the new adjacency are definitive. The metric ends at this local Adj-LSA construction. Synchronisation and FIB convergence use separate endpoints. Service-Path LSA Lead is measured on one router for one producer Adj-LSA version. It is the time of the ordinary NLSR Sync notification minus the arrival time of the service-prioritised Adj-LSA Data. A positive value means the service-prioritised copy arrives first. For each hand-off, the event-level Service-Path LSA Lead is the median across matched routers. The lead is an LSA-arrival difference, not a difference in LSDB installation time or FIB installation time.

\subsubsection{Routing-Control Parameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:evaluation:extended:sensitivity}

The main evaluation uses a 1~s SPRC Sync-publication delay and a 50~ms EDRC verification timeout. We vary each parameter independently while holding the other at its main-evaluation setting. Every cell uses the complete OptoFlood system, with Immediate Service Recovery, Service-Prioritised Routing Convergence, and Event-Driven Routing Convergence enabled. The topology, application workload, and 20~ms request interval follow the main experiment. Each parameter value is evaluated in one run with eight hand-offs. All cells replay the same attachment sequence and irregular interval schedule, giving matched observations for corresponding hand-offs across parameter values.

\textbf{SPRC Sync-publication delay.}
After an updated Adj-LSA has been installed locally and made available through SPRC along the service-relevant branch, this parameter delays its publication through ordinary NLSR Sync. The tested values are 0, 1, 2, and 4~s. The 1~s value is the main-evaluation setting, and the sweep characterises the effect of this parameter across the tested range. At 0~s, SPRC remains enabled and the updated Adj-LSA is published through ordinary NLSR Sync without an additional deferral.

Figure~\ref{fig:ext_sync_delay} shows the three routing timings. The median Service-Path LSA Lead increases from approximately 186.6~ms at 0~s to approximately 4.12~s at 4~s. Service-Path FIB convergence remains approximately 252--257~ms across the sweep, and Complete Mobility-Topology Update Latency stays approximately 165--168~ms. Network-Wide FIB convergence increases from approximately 491.6~ms at 0~s to approximately 4.47~s at 4~s. Increasing the publication delay extends the temporal head start before the update is disseminated more widely through ordinary NLSR Sync. Network-wide convergence shifts by approximately the added publication delay, while the service-path convergence time changes little. The 0~s case still has a positive Service-Path LSA Lead of about 187~ms, consistent with SPRC remaining active without deliberate Sync deferral.

\subsubsection{Multiple Consumers}
\label{sec:evaluation:extended:consumers}

We attach several consumers to the access network under two demand patterns. In the \emph{shared} pattern, all consumers view the same live stream. Two mechanisms then let them share the recovery: consumers requesting the same frame at the same time are aggregated in the PIT, and consumers lagging the live edge by less than the Data freshness period are served from the Content Store. The temporary forwarding state that OptoFlood instals from mobility-marked data is a per-prefix forwarder state, so it is shared as well. In the \emph{distinct} pattern, each consumer requests a separate stream from the same producer, so no content is shared, and each stream is recovered on its own prefix. We scale the number of consumers, and for the shared pattern we also vary the lag behind the live edge relative to the freshness period.

In Figure~\ref{fig:ext_consumers}, panel~(a) reports the shared-pattern per-consumer interruption time against consumer lag, in units of the Data freshness period. While the lag stays below the freshness period, the Content Store holds the requested frame and the consumer sees little disruption. Once the lag exceeds the freshness period, the cached frame expires and the disruption returns to the live-edge value. The curves for different numbers of consumers overlap, because PIT aggregation and the shared TFIB keep the per-consumer disruption independent of the number of consumers. Panel~(b) reports the recovery cost compared to the number of consumers for both patterns. Under the shared pattern, the cost stays close to its single-consumer value, because the mobility-marked Data is flooded once per event and its copies populate the Content Store. Under the distinct pattern the cost grows with the number of consumers, because each separate stream carries its own pending Interests and is recovered on its own prefix. The contrast isolates the saving from content sharing: the per consumer cost falls with scale under the shared pattern and remains constant per consumer under the distinct pattern.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The management of producer mobility in NDN has attracted significant research attention \cite{abrar:2023:systematic, feng:2016:survey, zhang:2016:mobility-survey}. Various solutions have emerged, generally categorised according to their underlying mechanisms. Here, we summarise three representative approaches—KITE, AFIRM, and MAP-Me—and highlight their respective strengths and limitations.

\textbf{KITE} \cite{zhang:2018:kite} is a trace-based mobility support scheme that leverages an immobile rendezvous point and the stateful NDN forwarding plane. A mobile producer maintains soft state by exchanging authenticated Interest/Data messages that establish a hop-by-hop trace toward its latest attachment point, allowing subsequent Interests to follow the trace after reaching the rendezvous point. This approach reduces the reliance on slow global routing convergence and keeps data retrieval largely transparent to applications. However, KITE introduces a dependency on a reachable rendezvous point and periodic signalling to refresh soft state, which can become a scalability concern under frequent mobility or large numbers of mobile producers.

\textbf{AFIRM (Adaptive Forwarding-based Link Recovery for Mobility)} \cite{meddeb:2018:afirm} addresses producer mobility by repairing forwarding behaviour through adaptive forwarding and local recovery, aiming to re-establish usable paths without introducing a permanent anchor. By reacting to mobility-induced disruptions and adjusting forwarding decisions, AFIRM can reduce service interruption for localised micro-mobility. However, its effectiveness depends on the timely detection and stability of local recovery in highly dynamic conditions; frequent mobility may also introduce additional signalling and forwarding overhead.

\textbf{MAP-Me (Managing Anchor-less Producer Mobility)} \cite{auge:2016:map-me, auge:2018:map-me} provides anchor-less mobility support by handling producer location updates in the data plane. Upon producer movement, MAP-Me propagates update information hop-by-hop to re-direct affected forwarding paths, avoiding the delays associated with purely routing-plane convergence. MAP-Me is therefore well-suited to latency-sensitive micro-mobility scenarios. However, in frequent or high-intensity mobility, repeated updates can generate noticeable overhead and may require careful tuning to balance responsiveness against network cost.

In terms of route updates, KITE maintains soft-state traces rooted at a rendezvous point, while AFIRM focusses on local forwarding recovery, and MAP-Me proactively updates forwarding state in the data plane upon movement. All three differ fundamentally from baseline NDN deployments where producer relocation requires routing-plane updates (e.g., NLSR prefix re-advertisement) and subsequent global convergence.

\textbf{MoQ/QUICR (Media over QUIC)} \cite{jennings:2022:quicr-arch} proposes a relay-assisted publish/subscribe architecture over QUIC for low-latency media delivery. Producers publish named media objects, and consumers subscribe; relays can cache and fan out objects. Mobility is handled primarily via endpoint re-attachment (e.g., QUIC connection migration and re-subscription), offering fast application-level continuity. However, this is an overlay design: it does not rely on the PIT/ FIF state of NDN in the network, nor does it provide an in the network mechanism to rescue pending requests along disrupted forwarding paths after producer movement. Thus, MoQ/QUICR shares NDN-like ideas (naming and caching), but targets a different layer and problem scope.

In comparison, our OptoFlood solution offers a flood-based mechanism specifically designed for dynamic producer mobility scenarios, employing controlled flooding of \emph{both} Data and Interest packets immediately following producer movement. Unlike KITE, OptoFlood does not depend on a rendezvous/anchor node, avoiding central dependency in the forwarding workflow. Compared to AFIRM and MAP-Me, which focus on forwarding recovery or explicit update propagation, OptoFlood prioritises \emph{immediate} temporary reachability: (i) mobility-marked Data packets are locally flooded to rescue pending Interests on the old path, and (ii) forwarders trigger scoped Interest flooding when forwarding state is missing, to rapidly discover the producer’s new attachment point. Once a working face is confirmed, forwarders install short-lived TFIB state to quickly cut back to unicast. Finally, OptoFlood couples these temporary paths with the routing plane by triggering a short-lived, locally scoped Fast-LSA update via NLSR, accelerating convergence while allowing normal NLSR advertisements to eventually take over.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \textit{OptoFlood}, a controllable flooding mechanism for producer mobility in Named Data Networking. OptoFlood addresses the key weakness of routing-based mobility handling: after a producer handover, forwarding state and routing information can remain stale for seconds, during which Interests may be discarded and Data may fail to return along invalidated reverse paths. Rather than waiting for global convergence, OptoFlood provides immediate localised recovery by establishing temporary bidirectional reachability and then accelerating routing convergence through lightweight integration with NLSR.

OptoFlood combines two complementary mechanisms. First, mobility-marked \emph{Data packet flooding} disseminates Data generated around a handover with bounded scope and duplicate suppression, improving the likelihood that pending Interests issued before the move are satisfied without relying on global route updates. Second, \emph{Interest packet flooding} is triggered by forwarders only as a last resort, when both FIB and TFIB provide no usable next hop, and is confined by native hop limits and per-node de-duplication. Intermediate forwarders opportunistically install short-lived TFIB hints to quickly revert subsequent Interests back to unicast forwarding once a viable face is observed. Finally, OptoFlood couples these temporary paths to the control plane via a Fast-LSA trigger, installing short-lived routing entries through NLSR to shorten the overall convergence period while allowing standard NLSR advertisements to eventually take over.

Overall, OptoFlood aims to keep mobility recovery \emph{local, short-lived, and explicitly bounded}: flooding is scoped (hop-limited), suppressed (duplicate filtering), and constrained (rate limiting), while cryptographic integrity is preserved by keeping immutable identifiers inside signed Data MetaInfo extensions and leaving hop-by-hop controls in the LP header for in-network processing. These design choices make OptoFlood practical for latency-sensitive micro-mobility settings, where rapid continuity matters and mobility events occur frequently enough that waiting for global convergence alone is insufficient.

\subsection{Long Term Impact Assessment}

\subsubsection{Continuous optimisation of network performance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Efficiency Improvements:} Because OptoFlood restores temporary reachability quickly after handover, it can reduce the duration of ``blind'' retransmission phases where consumers repeatedly re-express Interests while forwarding state is stale. Over time, this can translate into smoother application performance and fewer bursts of recovery traffic during mobility.
    \item \textbf{Enhanced Reliability:} By providing a mechanism to rescue pending Interests and by enabling forwarders to cache short-lived forwarding hints (TFIB), the network becomes more resilient to transient disruptions caused by producer relocation, especially when mobility remains local (within a few hops) and frequent.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Resource Usage and Network Stability}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resource Optimization:} OptoFlood is based on explicitly bounded flooding with a short lifetime. TFIB entries and Fast-LSA updates are also short-lived, limiting long-term state growth and reducing the risk of persistent routing pollution. This provides a pragmatic balance between rapid recovery and overhead.
    \item \textbf{Stability Analysis:} Although flooding is controlled, its interaction with retransmission behaviour, caching, and routing updates can influence stability under high load or dense mobility. The throttling and de-duplication mechanisms in both the forwarding plane (FloodId/Nonce caches, rate limits) and the routing plane (Fast-LSA throttling) are therefore essential to prevent oscillation and excessive control traffic, and should be considered carefully in larger deployments.
\end{itemize}
